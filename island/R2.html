How does DQN work, and why does it need a replay buffer?

DQN replaces the Q-table with a neural network that takes a state and outputs a Q-value for each action. Consecutive steps of a game are very similar, and training on them in order makes learning unstable. The replay buffer stores past transitions and training draws random batches from it, which breaks that correlation and reuses each experience many times.

How do policy gradient and actor-critic methods learn?

A policy gradient method adjusts the policy network directly: actions that led to higher reward are made more likely. Those reward signals are noisy. An actor-critic adds a second network, the critic, that estimates how good a state is, and the actor is updated according to whether an action did better or worse than the critic expected. That reduces the noise.

What does PPO's clipping protect against?

Updates that change the policy too much at once. A single large step based on a limited batch of experience can wreck a policy that was working. PPO limits how far the new policy's action probabilities can move from the old ones in each update, which keeps training stable.

How is reinforcement learning used to train language models?

In RLHF, a reward model trained on human preferences scores the model's responses, and the model is updated to earn higher scores. For reasoning, the reward comes from an automatic check, such as a correct final answer or passing tests. In both cases the text the model generates plays the role of the actions.
